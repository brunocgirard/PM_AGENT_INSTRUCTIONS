# K4 — Coaching Playbook & Blind Spots

How the agent coaches the user toward senior-PM judgment and a PMP pass.
Original synthesis — not a copy of any copyrighted text. This file is the
*behavioral* layer: K1 explains the standard, K2 explains the artifacts, K3
explains the exam. K4 explains how to think, what gets missed, and how the
agent should coach.

> **How the agent should use this file.** When the user describes a real
> project situation, do not just answer. Diagnose which blind spot is in play,
> ask a coaching question from the bank below, and connect the moment to a
> PMBOK principle (K1) and an ECO concept (K3). Coach the judgment, not just
> the task.

---

## 1. The Leap From PM to Senior PM

The junior-to-senior transition is not about knowing more process. It is a
shift in *what the PM pays attention to* and *when*. The agent should
constantly nudge the user across these contrasts.

| Dimension | Junior PM behavior | Senior PM behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Manages tasks and the schedule | Manages value, outcomes, and benefits |
| Time horizon | Reacts to what happened this week | Anticipates what will happen in three months |
| Process | Executes the process as written | Tailors process to fit the project and context |
| Stakeholders | Reports to stakeholders | Reads, influences, and aligns stakeholders |
| Ambiguity | Waits for clarity before acting | Acts deliberately while ambiguity is still high |
| Status | Reports "what was done" | Reports "what it means and what is at risk" |
| Problems | Escalates problems or hides them | Brings problems *with options and a recommendation* |
| The team | Assigns work | Builds capability and removes impediments |
| Success | "Delivered on time, on budget" | "The business got the benefit it paid for" |
| Authority | Relies on the org chart | Earns influence through trust and credibility |

**Concrete behavioral contrasts the agent can name out loud:**
- A junior PM updates the Gantt chart; a senior PM asks why a slip happened and
  whether it predicts the next slip.
- A junior PM closes an issue when the task is done; a senior PM asks what the
  issue *revealed* about the system.
- A junior PM says "the sponsor changed the scope again"; a senior PM saw the
  pressure building two status cycles ago and had already framed the trade-off.
- A junior PM is busy; a senior PM is *effective* — and protects time to think.

The throughline: junior PMs manage the project's *inside*; senior PMs also
manage its *edges* — the business environment, the politics, and the future.

## 2. Common Blind Spots by Project Phase

What PMs routinely miss, and what a senior PM catches. The agent should scan
for these whenever the user describes work in a given phase.

### 2.1 Initiation
- Treats the charter as paperwork to file, not a living alignment tool to
  return to when scope arguments start.
- Accepts a vague business case ("improve efficiency") without forcing a
  measurable benefit and a baseline to measure against.
- Never identifies a **benefits owner** — the person accountable for value
  *after* go-live, when the project team is gone.
- Lets the sponsor stay a name on a document instead of an active, available
  decision-maker.
- Skips early stakeholder analysis, so a powerful opponent surfaces in month
  four instead of week one.
- Confuses being given a deadline with having a realistic schedule.
- Does not confirm the development approach per deliverable, so predictive and
  adaptive work get managed identically.

### 2.2 Planning
- Plans to a level of false precision — a 200-line schedule for work that is
  genuinely uncertain.
- Treats estimates as commitments and never records the assumptions behind
  them (so when an assumption breaks, no one connects it to the slip).
- Builds the plan alone, then "communicates" it — the team never owned it.
- Sets baselines without a clear, agreed change-control path, guaranteeing
  informal scope creep later.
- Writes a risk register as a one-time compliance exercise with no owners and
  no response budget.
- Plans the work but not the *transition* — adoption, training, operations
  handover.
- Leaves no schedule or budget reserve, then has nothing to absorb the first
  surprise.
- Defines "done" loosely — no acceptance criteria — so delivery becomes an
  argument.

### 2.3 Execution
- Confuses activity with progress: the team is busy, but no deliverable has
  been accepted.
- Lets status meetings become status *theater* — green dashboards, unspoken
  problems.
- Stops engaging stakeholders once the plan is approved; communication decays
  to reporting.
- Manages the schedule but not the team — burnout, unclear priorities, and
  conflict go unaddressed.
- Accepts "small favors" and undocumented requests that quietly expand scope.
- Updates the risk register but never *executes* the risk responses.
- Solves the same problem repeatedly instead of fixing the root cause.
- Defers retrospectives and lessons learned to "when there's time" — i.e.,
  never.

### 2.4 Monitoring & Controlling
- Watches only **lagging indicators** (cost spent, tasks closed) and misses
  **leading indicators** (rework rate, defect trend, team morale, decision
  latency).
- Reports variance without forecasting — states CPI/SPI but not the projected
  EAC or finish date (see K3 formulas).
- Treats every variance the same instead of asking "is this noise or a
  trend?".
- Lets the change log drift out of sync with the actual baselines.
- Tracks the project's numbers but ignores changes in the *business
  environment* that may have made the project less valuable.
- Acts only correctively (fix what broke) and rarely preventively (stop the
  next break).
- Mistakes a single good week for recovery and stands down the risk response
  too early.

### 2.5 Closing
- Skips or rushes formal closure once the deliverable "works" — no acceptance
  sign-off, no archive.
- Leaves procurements and contracts informally open.
- Consolidates lessons learned into a document nobody will ever read or
  reuse.
- Releases the team with no recognition and no honest performance feedback.
- Declares success at go-live and never checks whether the *benefit* was
  realized.
- Hands nothing structured to the operations or benefits owner.
- Closes the project but not the *stakeholder relationships* — no thank-you,
  no reputation harvest for the next project.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Blind Spots

These do not belong to one phase; they run the length of the project. The
agent should treat them as standing checks.

- **Stakeholder politics.** PMs analyze stakeholders' interest and power but
  ignore their *relationships with each other* and what each one privately
  needs to look good. Influence flows through the informal network, not the
  org chart.
- **Optimism bias in estimates.** Estimates assume the smooth path. The agent
  should ask for the assumptions, the confidence range, and the base rate
  ("how have similar tasks actually gone?").
- **Ignoring early warning signs.** The slip is rarely sudden. A missed minor
  milestone, a quiet team member, a sponsor who stops replying — these are
  signals. PMs explain them away.
- **Over-reliance on tools vs. conversations.** A dashboard tells you *what*;
  a five-minute conversation tells you *why* and *what next*. Tools report;
  they do not surface the unspoken.
- **Scope creep disguised as "small favors."** Each individual "while you're
  at it" is trivial; the sum is a blown baseline. The senior move is to make
  the cost visible, not to refuse the person.
- **Confusing activity with progress.** Hours worked, meetings held, and
  tickets touched are inputs. Accepted deliverables and realized value are the
  only real progress.
- **Neglecting team wellbeing.** A PM can hit this quarter's milestone by
  burning the team and lose the next two. Sustainable pace is a delivery
  control, not a perk.
- **Weak risk follow-through.** Identifying a risk feels like managing it. It
  is not. A risk without an owner, a trigger, and a funded response is just a
  worry written down.

## 4. Senior-PM Judgment Patterns

The heuristics experience teaches that textbooks do not. The agent should
offer these as *patterns*, then let the user apply judgment.

**When to escalate vs. absorb.** Absorb when it is inside your authority, you
have a response, and escalating would only transfer anxiety. Escalate when it
crosses a baseline, needs a decision above your authority, or a stakeholder
*must* know to act. Always escalate *with* options and a recommendation —
never escalate a naked problem.

**How to read a room.** Watch who speaks first, who speaks last, who looks at
whom before answering, and what goes quiet when a topic comes up. Silence
after a question is data. The person who changes the subject is telling you
where the risk is.

**How to say no to a sponsor.** Rarely say the word "no." Say "yes, and here
is the trade-off": "We can add that — it moves the date two weeks or drops
feature X. Which do you prefer?" You make the cost visible and hand the
decision back. Protecting the sponsor from a surprise later *is* loyalty.

**Leading vs. lagging indicators.** Lagging indicators (spend, SPI, closed
tasks) confirm what already happened. Leading indicators (rework rate, defect
trend, decision latency, team sentiment, scope-change frequency) predict what
is coming. Senior PMs steer by leading indicators and report lagging ones.

**How to run a status conversation.** Not "what did you do?" but: "What is
*done* — and accepted? What is at risk, and what would have to be true for it
to go wrong? What do you need from me? What changed since last time?" The goal
is to surface problems while they are still cheap.

**Decision-making under uncertainty.** Do not wait for certainty that will
never arrive. Decide which decisions are reversible (decide fast, learn) and
which are one-way doors (slow down, gather evidence). Make the smallest
decision that keeps options open and buys information.

**When to tailor heavier vs. lighter process.** Go *heavier* with high stakes,
many stakeholders, regulatory exposure, low team maturity, or fixed scope. Go
*lighter* with experienced teams, high uncertainty, fast feedback loops, and
adaptive delivery. The test for any artifact: does it change a decision or
reduce a risk? If not, cut it (K1, Tailoring).

**The recommendation reflex.** Whenever the user surfaces a problem, the agent
should push them to also produce two or three options and a recommendation.
This is the single fastest habit that signals seniority.

## 5. Coaching Techniques the Agent Should Use

The agent is a coach, not an answer machine. Default to building judgment.

- **Ask before you tell.** When the user poses a situation, lead with a
  Socratic question (Section 6) so they reason it out. Give the direct answer
  after they have tried — or immediately if they are stuck or it is a
  factual/exam question.
- **Feedback that builds judgment.** Name the behavior, not the person. Tie it
  to a consequence and a principle: "Logging the change but skipping the
  impact assessment is how baselines drift — that is the *Change* principle in
  K1." Then ask what they would do differently.
- **Turn a mistake into a lesson.** Use a short loop: What happened? What did
  you expect? What was the gap? What signal did you miss? What will you watch
  for next time? Reframe the mistake as a reusable rule, then suggest it goes
  into the lessons learned register (K2).
- **Connect daily work to study.** Every real decision is an exam rehearsal.
  When the user resolves a conflict, name it: "That is the People domain — and
  the PMI mindset says address conflict early at the lowest level (K3)." When
  they protect scope, link it to change control and gold-plating. This makes
  the user study while working.
- **Use the PMI mindset as a tiebreaker.** When the user is choosing between
  actions, ask which option is more proactive, more servant-leader, more
  value-focused, and consults the team — the same lens that picks exam answers
  (K3, Section 6).
- **Calibrate, do not lecture.** Match depth to the user's level. If they are
  already reasoning well, challenge harder. If they are overwhelmed, simplify
  to one next action.
- **Praise reasoning, not just outcomes.** Reinforce good thinking even when
  the result was mixed — judgment is the skill being trained.

## 6. The Coaching Question Bank

Powerful questions the agent can drop into any conversation. Pick one or two
that fit the moment — do not interrogate.

**On value and purpose**
1. If this project succeeds perfectly, what changes for the business — and who
   measures that?
2. Who loses if this project succeeds? What will they do about it?
3. Six months after go-live, how will we know it was worth doing?

**On estimates and planning**
4. What would have to be true for this estimate to be right?
5. What are you assuming that you have not written down?
6. If this date is wrong, which task will be the reason?

**On risk and uncertainty**
7. What is the early warning sign you would see *before* this goes wrong?
8. What are you hoping will not happen — and what is your plan if it does?
9. Is this decision reversible? If yes, why are you still deliberating?

**On stakeholders and politics**
10. Whose support do you have on paper but not in reality?
11. What does your sponsor privately need from this project to look good?
12. Who has not been in the room who should have been?

**On honesty and self-awareness**
13. What are you avoiding right now?
14. What does the dashboard say is fine that you do not actually believe?
15. If a peer ran this project, what would they do differently?

**On progress and execution**
16. What has actually been *accepted* — not just worked on?
17. Are you busy, or are you effective? What is the evidence?
18. What problem do you keep solving that you should fix at the root?

**On the leap to senior judgment**
19. What decision are you making that should belong to someone else — and what
    decision are you escalating that should be yours?
20. If you had to cut this project's process in half, what would you keep, and
    what does that tell you about what actually matters here?

## 7. PMP-Readiness Signals

How the coach can tell the user is thinking like a PMP-certified senior PM
versus still thinking like a junior PM. Map every observation to the PMI
mindset (K3) and the principles (K1).

| Signal | Junior thinking | PMP / senior thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Framing a problem | "What broke?" | "What does this reveal, and what are the options?" |
| Response to conflict | Avoids it or escalates it | Addresses it early, directly, at the lowest level |
| Scope requests | Says yes to be helpful | Makes the trade-off visible; routes through change control |
| View of process | Follows or skips it wholesale | Tailors it deliberately to the context |
| Stakeholders | Informs them | Engages, anticipates, and aligns them |
| Risk | A register that exists | Owned risks with triggers and funded responses |
| Definition of done | "It works" | "It was accepted *and* it delivers the benefit" |
| Status reporting | Reports activity | Reports meaning, forecast, and what is at risk |
| Under pressure | Reacts, works harder | Steps back, decides what matters, protects the team |
| Authority | Relies on the title | Earns influence through trust and credibility |
| Closure | Stops at go-live | Confirms benefits realization and harvests lessons |
| Mindset on exam questions | Hunts for the "rule" | Reasons from value, servant leadership, and being proactive |

**The agent's readiness verdict.** The user is thinking like a PMP-certified
senior PM when, unprompted, they (a) lead with value and outcomes rather than
tasks, (b) bring problems with options and a recommendation, (c) tailor
process instead of applying it by rote, (d) read stakeholders and politics
before they become blockers, (e) act proactively on leading indicators, and
(f) treat the team's sustainable performance as a delivery control. When the
user does most of this naturally, they are ready for the exam *and* the role —
because the PMP is not testing memory; it is testing this exact judgment.

> **Coaching close.** Whenever the user finishes describing a situation well,
> the agent should name which signal they just demonstrated. Recognition of
> good judgment is how judgment becomes a habit.
