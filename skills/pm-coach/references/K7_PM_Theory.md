# K7 — Classic PM Theory & Frameworks

A study reference covering the classic project-management theory the PMP exam
draws on. Original synthesis — not a copy of any copyrighted text, and not real
exam questions.

> **How to use this file.** The exam rarely asks "what is Theory X?" It puts you
> in a scenario and expects you to *act* on the theory. Learn each model well
> enough to recognize it from a story and pick the right next action. See the
> closing section, "How theory shows up on the exam."

---

## 1. Team Development — Tuckman's Stages

Bruce Tuckman's model describes the predictable phases a team moves through as
it forms and matures. A team can also slip backward (for example, a new member
or a new problem can push a "performing" team back into "storming").

| Stage | What it looks like | PM actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Polite, uncertain; members rely on the PM; roles unclear | Provide direction, set goals, clarify roles, establish ground rules |
| **Storming** | Conflict over approach, power, and ideas; frustration surfaces | Coach, facilitate, surface and resolve conflict, keep focus on goals |
| **Norming** | Norms settle; trust and collaboration grow; members support each other | Step back, let the team self-organize, reinforce good behavior |
| **Performing** | High-performing, autonomous, productive; minimal supervision needed | Delegate, empower, remove impediments, get out of the way |
| **Adjourning** | Project ends; team disbands; reflection and release | Recognize contributions, capture lessons learned, support transition |

**Exam angle.** Questions describe team behavior and ask what stage it is in, or
what the PM should do. Match the behavior to the stage, then match the PM's
*leadership style* to that stage: **directive in forming, coaching in storming,
supportive in norming, delegating in performing.** A common trap: a team in
storming is normal and healthy — do not assume the team is broken or that
someone should be removed.

## 2. Conflict Management

Conflict is inevitable and is not inherently bad — handled well, it improves
decisions. The exam tests both *sources* of conflict and the *five modes* of
resolving it (the Thomas–Kilmann model).

### Common sources of conflict
Schedules, project priorities, resources, technical opinions, administrative
procedures, cost, and — least often — personality. Note: research cited by PMI
shows schedules and priorities cause more conflict than personality clashes.

### The five conflict-resolution modes

| Mode | Description | When acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| **Collaborate / Problem-solve** | Work the issue together to a consensus that satisfies everyone; address the root cause | Almost always preferred; best when relationship and outcome both matter and time allows |
| **Compromise / Reconcile** | Each side gives up something; "lose-lose" or partial-win | When parties have equal power, or a quick partial resolution is needed |
| **Smooth / Accommodate** | Emphasize agreement, downplay differences; yield to the other party | To preserve harmony, when the issue is minor, or when you are wrong |
| **Force / Direct** | Impose one viewpoint; "win-lose" | Emergencies, safety, when a quick decisive call is essential, or when you must enforce policy |
| **Withdraw / Avoid** | Retreat or postpone; do not address the issue now | When stakes are low, you need to cool off, gather facts, or others can resolve it better |

**The PMI mindset prefers Collaborate / Problem-solve** — it produces durable,
win-win outcomes and treats the cause, not the symptom. **Withdraw/Avoid is the
least desirable** as a default. But the "best" answer is situational: in a true
emergency, *force* may be correct; if you need facts before deciding, a brief
*withdraw* can be right.

**Exam angle.** Resolve conflict **directly, early, and at the lowest possible
level** — between the people involved, not by escalating immediately. If two
answer choices are both reasonable, pick the more collaborative one. Watch for
disguised wording: "let's split the difference" = compromise; "let's not worry
about it" = avoid; "I'll decide, this is final" = force.

## 3. Motivation Theories

The exam expects you to recognize each theory and apply it to a team scenario.

| Theory | Core idea | Exam angle |
|---|---|---|
| **Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs** | Needs are layered: physiological → safety → social/belonging → esteem → self-actualization; lower needs must be met before higher ones motivate | People are motivated by the lowest *unmet* need; you cannot motivate with esteem if job security is in doubt |
| **Herzberg — Two-Factor (Motivation-Hygiene)** | *Hygiene factors* (salary, conditions, policy, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate; *motivators* (achievement, recognition, growth, the work itself) create satisfaction | Fixing pay/conditions only removes unhappiness; to *motivate*, add responsibility, recognition, and growth |
| **McGregor — Theory X / Theory Y** | Theory X: people dislike work and need control; Theory Y: people are self-motivated and seek responsibility | Modern PMI thinking favors Theory Y — trust, empower, delegate; Theory X management is a red flag |
| **McClelland — Acquired Needs (Three Needs)** | People are driven by **achievement**, **affiliation**, or **power**; one usually dominates | Assign work to fit the dominant need — challenging goals for achievers, team roles for affiliators, leadership for power-motivated people |
| **Vroom — Expectancy Theory** | Motivation = Expectancy (effort leads to performance) × Instrumentality (performance leads to reward) × Valence (the reward is valued) | People work hard only if they believe effort pays off *and* the reward matters to them; remove any one factor and motivation collapses |

**Exam angle.** A scenario describes an unmotivated team; identify which theory
the situation illustrates and pick the matching intervention. Recognize that
**money is a hygiene factor (Herzberg), not a true motivator** — a frequently
tested point.

## 4. Leadership & Management

### Leadership vs. management
**Management** is about doing things right — administering systems, processes,
and controls to deliver predictable results. **Leadership** is about doing the
right things — setting direction, aligning people, inspiring, and enabling
change. A good PM does both, but the modern exam leans heavily toward
*leadership*, especially **servant leadership**.

### Leadership styles

| Style | Description |
|---|---|
| **Servant** | Serves the team first; removes impediments, develops people, shields the team. PMI's favored model |
| **Transformational** | Inspires through vision, motivation, and intellectual stimulation; drives change and growth |
| **Transactional** | Motivates through rewards and consequences tied to goals; focuses on routine and exchange |
| **Charismatic** | Energizes through personality, enthusiasm, and confidence; reliant on the individual |
| **Laissez-faire** | "Hands-off"; lets the team self-direct. Effective with mature, expert teams; risky with new teams |
| **Directive** | Tells the team what to do and how; clear but low autonomy |
| **Supportive** | Provides help, encouragement, and resources; focuses on team well-being |

### Situational leadership
There is no single best style. **Effective leaders adapt their style to the
team's maturity and the situation** — directive with an inexperienced team,
delegating with a seasoned one. This ties directly to Tuckman (Section 1).

### Emotional intelligence (EI)
The ability to recognize and manage emotions — your own and others'. Commonly
described in four (sometimes five) components:

- **Self-awareness** — recognizing your own emotions and their effect.
- **Self-management (self-regulation)** — controlling impulses and reactions.
- **Social awareness (empathy)** — reading others' emotions and group dynamics.
- **Relationship management (social skills)** — influencing, coaching, and
  resolving conflict; sometimes split out as a separate component, with
  *motivation* listed as a fifth.

**Exam angle.** EI is heavily tested in the People domain. The "best" answer in
an interpersonal scenario is usually the one showing empathy, self-control, and
listening *before* acting. A PM who reacts emotionally or ignores team feelings
is the wrong choice.

## 5. Power Types

The forms of power a PM can draw on. The first three come from the PM's position
and rank; the last two are earned by the person.

| Power type | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Positional / Formal (legitimate)** | The PM's role and authority | Comes with the title; weak on its own |
| **Reward** | Ability to give desirable outcomes (bonus, recognition, choice assignments) | Effective when rewards are valued and within the PM's control |
| **Coercive / Penalty** | Ability to punish or impose negative consequences | Use sparingly; breeds resentment and fear — generally to be avoided |
| **Expert** | The PM's knowledge, skill, and credibility | Highly effective; earned, not granted |
| **Referent** | Respect, trust, and the desire of others to be associated with the PM | Highly effective; based on personal credibility and relationships |

**Exam angle.** **Expert** and **referent** power are the most effective and the
PMI-preferred bases for influence. **Coercive/penalty** power is the least
desirable — avoid it as a default. Reward and formal power are situationally
useful. A new PM joining an established team typically has only *formal* power
at first and must *build* expert and referent power over time.

## 6. Negotiation

Negotiation appears in stakeholder agreements, resource assignments, scope
trade-offs, and procurement.

### Principled negotiation
A "win-win" approach. Its core ideas:

- **Separate the people from the problem** — attack the issue, not the person.
- **Focus on interests, not positions** — ask *why* each side wants something.
- **Invent options for mutual gain** — expand the pie before dividing it.
- **Insist on objective criteria** — use fair standards (market rate, data,
  precedent) rather than willpower.

### BATNA
**Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement** — your fallback if no deal is
reached. Knowing your BATNA tells you when to walk away; a strong BATNA gives
negotiating power. Never accept a deal worse than your BATNA.

### Win-win
PMI favors **win-win** outcomes that preserve the relationship. Win-lose and
lose-lose results damage long-term collaboration and are rarely the best answer.

### Tactics to recognize
Be able to spot manipulative tactics: **deadline pressure**, **lowball/highball
offers**, **good cop / bad cop**, **fait accompli** ("it's already decided"),
**missing person** ("I have to check with my boss"), **withdrawal**, and
**unreasonable extreme demands**. Recognizing a tactic lets you stay calm,
refocus on interests and objective criteria, and avoid being pressured.

**Exam angle.** The right answer keeps the relationship intact, seeks mutual
gain, and relies on facts and fair standards — not on pressure or threats.

## 7. Procurement & Contracts

### The procurement life cycle
From the buyer's perspective, procurement runs through four stages:

1. **Plan procurement** — decide *what* to buy and *how*. Includes the
   **make-or-buy analysis** (is it cheaper/safer to build it in-house or buy it
   externally?) and selecting contract types.
2. **Conduct procurement** — obtain seller responses, evaluate bids, and award
   contracts. Includes **source selection** — choosing sellers against criteria
   such as cost, capability, technical approach, references, and risk.
3. **Control procurement** — manage the relationship and the contract:
   **contract administration**, monitoring seller performance, processing
   changes, inspecting deliverables, and handling claims.
4. **Close procurement** — confirm work is complete and accepted, settle final
   payments and claims, and formally close each contract.

### Key concepts
- **Make-or-buy analysis** weighs cost, capability, capacity, risk, and
  strategic factors.
- **Source selection criteria** must be defined *before* evaluating bids, to
  keep the choice objective and defensible.
- **Contract administration** ensures both parties meet their obligations; the
  contract is a formal, legally binding document — changes must be controlled.
- A **claim** is a disputed change; if unresolved, it may go to negotiation,
  alternative dispute resolution, or arbitration/litigation.

### Contract types — math is in K5
The exam tests **fixed-price** (FFP, FPIF, FP-EPA), **cost-reimbursable**
(CPFF, CPIF, CPAF), and **time-and-materials (T&M)** contracts, plus risk
allocation between buyer and seller. **For contract calculations** — point of
total assumption, fee/incentive sharing, target vs. ceiling cost — **see K5 for
the contract math.** This file covers only the *theory* of how procurement
works.

**Exam angle.** Know who carries the cost risk in each contract type
(fixed-price = seller; cost-reimbursable = buyer) and that source selection
criteria are set up front.

## 8. Quality Theory

Quality is conformance to requirements and **fitness for use**. The exam draws
on the major quality thinkers and a set of standard tools.

### Key thinkers and concepts

| Thinker / concept | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Deming — PDCA** | The **Plan-Do-Check-Act** cycle of continuous improvement; quality is management's responsibility |
| **Juran — Fitness for use** | Quality means the product actually serves the customer's real need; the "quality trilogy" of planning, control, improvement |
| **Crosby — Cost of conformance** | "Quality is free" and **zero defects**; prevention is cheaper than failure; emphasized *cost of conformance vs. cost of nonconformance* |
| **Ishikawa** | The **cause-and-effect (fishbone / Ishikawa) diagram**; championed the basic quality tools |

### Cost of Quality (COQ)
The total cost of achieving quality, split into:

- **Cost of conformance** — money spent to *prevent* defects: prevention costs
  (training, good process) and appraisal costs (inspection, testing).
- **Cost of nonconformance** — money lost *because of* defects: internal failure
  (rework, scrap) and external failure (warranty, recalls, lost reputation).

**Prevention is cheaper than inspection**, and inspection is cheaper than
failure found by the customer. The PMI principle: **quality is planned and
built in, not inspected in.**

### The 7 basic quality tools
**Cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram, flowchart, check sheet, Pareto chart,
histogram, control chart, scatter diagram.** Know what each does — e.g., a
**Pareto chart** ranks causes by frequency and reflects the **80/20 rule** (most
problems come from a few causes); a **control chart** shows whether a process is
*in control* over time.

### Six Sigma / control limits basics
- **Six Sigma** is a data-driven method to reduce defects and variation.
- **Control limits** (upper/lower) are set by the *process itself* and show
  natural variation; **specification limits** come from the *customer/
  requirements*.
- A process is **out of control** when a point falls outside the control
  limits, or when the **rule of seven** applies — seven consecutive points on
  one side of the mean signal a non-random trend even if all are within limits.

**Exam angle.** Distinguish **control limits (process)** from **specification
limits (customer)**; recognize the **rule of seven**; and always favor
**prevention over inspection**.

## 9. Stakeholder & Communication Models

### Stakeholder analysis grids
Tools to classify stakeholders and decide how to engage each one:

- **Power/Interest grid** — plots stakeholders by their *power* (influence) and
  *interest* (concern). Four quadrants drive strategy:
  *high power / high interest* → **manage closely**;
  *high power / low interest* → **keep satisfied**;
  *low power / high interest* → **keep informed**;
  *low power / low interest* → **monitor** (minimal effort).
- Variants swap an axis: **power/influence** and **influence/impact** grids.
- **Salience model** — classifies stakeholders by three attributes: **power**,
  **legitimacy** (their involvement is appropriate), and **urgency** (they need
  immediate attention). Stakeholders with all three are the highest priority.

### Communication methods
- **Interactive** — real-time, multi-directional exchange (meetings, calls,
  video). Best for complex or sensitive topics; ensures shared understanding.
- **Push** — sent *to* recipients without confirming receipt (emails, reports,
  memos). Distributes information but does not guarantee it was read or
  understood.
- **Pull** — recipients access information *themselves* at will (intranet,
  knowledge base, dashboards). Best for large audiences and large content.

### The communication model
Communication is a process: a **sender encodes** a message, transmits it
through a **medium**, and a **receiver decodes** it; the receiver
**acknowledges** and gives **feedback**. **Noise** is anything that distorts the
message — language, jargon, distance, culture, technology, emotion. The sender
is responsible for making the message clear and confirming it was understood.

**Difference to keep straight.** Stakeholder grids decide **who** to engage and
**how intensively**; communication *methods* decide **how** to deliver
information; the communication *model* explains **why** messages get distorted
and how to reduce noise. The exam may test all three in one scenario.

**Exam angle.** For a sensitive or complex issue, choose **interactive**
communication — never resolve conflict or break bad news by email (push).

## 10. Ethics

PMI's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct rests on four values. You do not
need to memorize the document — you need to *act* on its spirit.

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Responsibility** | Own your decisions and their consequences; do only work you are qualified for; report unethical conduct; honor commitments |
| **Respect** | Treat people, resources, and the environment with regard; negotiate in good faith; do not abuse power; listen to other views |
| **Fairness** | Be objective and impartial; disclose conflicts of interest; do not discriminate or show favoritism; compete and award honestly |
| **Honesty** | Tell the truth; do not deceive or mislead; create an environment where the truth can be told; report accurate status |

**Exam angle.** Ethics questions are framed as dilemmas: a stakeholder asks you
to hide bad news, accept a gift, cut a corner, or misreport status. The correct
answer is almost always to **be transparent, disclose, refuse the unethical
request, and follow organizational policy and law** — even when it is
uncomfortable or unpopular. Never falsify reports, never accept inappropriate
gifts, and always disclose conflicts of interest. When laws or customs
conflict, follow the higher ethical standard and applicable law.

## How Theory Shows Up on the Exam

Theory is **tested through scenarios, not definitions.** The exam will not ask
"List Tuckman's stages." It will describe a team arguing over technical
approach (storming) and ask what you should do — and the right answer applies
the theory without naming it.

Practical guidance:

- **Recognize the model from the story.** A demotivated team after a pay raise
  failed to help → Herzberg (pay is hygiene, not a motivator). Members arguing
  over priorities → storming, plus a conflict-mode choice.
- **Convert theory into an action.** The answer is usually a verb: *facilitate,
  collaborate, listen, empower, disclose, build expert power.*
- **Default to the PMI mindset** (see K3, Section 6): collaborate over compete,
  prevent over inspect, lead as a servant, address conflict directly and early,
  communicate face-to-face for sensitive topics, and act ethically even at a
  cost.
- **Eliminate extreme answers.** Choices that force, punish, avoid, escalate
  prematurely, or hide information are usually wrong.
- **Match style to situation.** Situational leadership, the conflict modes, and
  communication methods are all "it depends" topics — read the scenario for
  urgency, stakes, team maturity, and relationship before choosing.

Study tip: for each model in this file, write one sentence describing a project
situation that would trigger it and one sentence on the action you would take.
That sentence pair is exactly what the exam expects you to produce under time
pressure.
